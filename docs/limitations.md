# Limitations and concurrency

Gibbus is designed for smooth univariate density modeling under per-component log-concavity. The following constraints and qualifications should be considered part of the model contract.


`gibbus` is a univariate density estimator for point or interval-censored observations; it is not a multivariate model. A finite-sample fit can approximate data generated by a heavy-tailed population, but log-concavity is still a modeling restriction and tail extrapolation should not be interpreted as a heavy-tail model. Automatic component-count selection uses an approximate KDE/BIC screening pipeline (deterministic by default, but still sensitive to subsampling); inspect `selection_diagnostics` or disable selection subsampling when that choice is scientifically material. Fitting is not safe to perform concurrently on the same `Distribution` instance, and opt-in `suppress_warnings=True` uses Python's process-global warning filters (context-local filters are the default only on free-threaded CPython builds).

## Numerical accuracy qualifications

**Tail resolution.** Quantiles below the reliable stored CDF range are solved directly from the fitted potential in log space rather than inferred from a CDF value that no longer carries significant tail digits. On unbounded supports this path is designed and regression-tested across the representable float64 probability range. Finite endpoints use the same numerical treatment; near a nonzero endpoint the eventual resolution limit is the spacing of representable public-coordinate floats.

Near a **finite** endpoint, boundary distances are evaluated directly in the public coordinate system rather than reconstructed from two internal coordinates. A lower endpoint at zero can therefore be approached through essentially the full positive float64 range; for nonzero endpoints, the only remaining floor is ordinary floating-point representability of the coordinate itself. Quantiles remain inside the support and monotone.

**Mixtures with narrow minority components.** Each component uses its own responsibility-weighted center and robust scale, so its basis and normalization work on that component's numerical scale. This keeps narrow or remote minority components well conditioned even when component widths or locations differ by many orders of magnitude. Mixture CDFs are constructed from the weighted component density, and component/mixture consistency is covered by the numerical regression suite.

Checking `error_estimate` after fitting a mixture is the simplest general guard. `uncertified_mass` (CDF panels whose nonnegativity could not be proved) distinguishes that case from a normalization or panel interpolation error. Note that a `cdf`/`ppf` round-trip is *not* a useful check on its own: the PPF is constructed from the CDF, so the two stay self-consistent even when both are wrong.

The basis is smooth: it has no `|x|` terms, nonsmooth knots, or fractional-power
bulk terms, so fitting populations with such features is a model approximation.

## Known open items

A failed explicit request raises an exception rather than silently installing an invalid result.

- **Data concentrated far from a finite end.** On a half-line or bounded support, data whose 10–90% spread is below 0.5% of the median's distance from the nearest finite end are rejected with a clear error (the fitting coordinate is anchored at the end). Fit on the full line, or on a support whose finite end is nearer the data. Data that hug the end itself are fine.
- **High explicit degrees on half-lines.** An explicit degree whose cold start does not converge is lifted from the converged smaller degrees, so degrees up to about 11 converge on ordinary half-line data. At degree 12 the Newton steps are ill-conditioned: a fit can stall short of its optimum (`non_descent`) depending on the last bits of the data or platform, and takes several to about 20 seconds when it succeeds. On data concentrated well away from the endpoint (spread of a few percent of the distance) explicit degrees of about 8 and above may still not converge (`non_descent`). Automatic degrees are reliable there.
- **High-degree polynomial tails.** Explicit `Polynomial` tails of degree 10 and above are numerically difficult.
- **Heavy tails and `SoftPower`.** An automatic `SoftPower` estimate that reaches `p = 1` reports that the tail is heavier than the family can represent; give an explicit power or another family.
- **Extreme-coordinate regimes.** Public-coordinate accuracy is limited by float64 representability; unusual offsets or scales may still cause an explicit numerical failure.
- **Mixture performance.** Mixture EM repeatedly fits component models, and near-degenerate curvature contacts can make cut exchanges slow. Automatic component selection is heuristic and may refine several neighboring counts.
- **Tail-family mismatch.** Explicit tail choices are not replaced automatically, even when statistically incompatible with the population; automatic comparison of different tail classes is not implemented.
- **Shapes on the edge of the convex cone.** The curvature search scales `q''` by the sum of its term magnitudes. Where every curvature term is at rounding level (an exactly linear potential with rounding-sized higher coefficients), that scaling turns rounding into a full-size violation, and a fit there can come back `unverified` on one platform and verified on another. Automatic span selection then keeps the smaller converged span.
- **Shape evidence.** Convexity is verified numerically within a threshold `tau` (recorded as `shape_tau` in `fit_diagnostics`), from interval enclosures and tail bounds; it is not an arbitrary-precision proof. `log_concavity_margin` is a separate grid diagnostic.
- **Degree-ladder history.** The single-component degree ladder records why it stopped in an internal selection history that is not yet exposed through `fit_diagnostics`; mixtures report their growth steps in `em_diagnostics["selection"]`.
- **Interval mixtures with gated tails.** Interval-censored mixtures whose components carry `SoftPower` or `Exponential` tails can take tens of seconds: each M-step's curvature contact moves with the coefficients, and the point cuts that enforce convexity follow it one exchange at a time.
- **Far-tail masses.** Survival and tail-quantile queries normally use the compiled tail integrator. Where it cannot close a far-tail row (a fit whose requested tail leader is fixed to zero, or a potential beyond binary64 resolution), the scaled tail quadrature evaluates the mass instead and the event is recorded in `suppressed_failures()`.
- **Platforms.** CI runs the suite on Linux, Windows and macOS for CPython 3.11 through 3.14; the local verification record is in [Development status](development-status.md). CI also tests the declared NumPy/SciPy floors; debug builds are not yet qualified.

## Concurrency

Evaluation of an already-fitted `Distribution` is read-only at the Python level and the compiled bulk kernels may release the GIL. `gibbus` itself does not create thread pools, process pools, or other worker orchestration during fitting or spectral finalization. Do not mutate, transform, load, or refit the same instance concurrently. Separate `Distribution` instances may be fitted concurrently by the caller, but keep `suppress_warnings=False`: Python warning filters are process-global on standard CPython builds, 3.14 included, so opt-in warning suppression can affect unrelated threads while its context is active. The suppressed-failure ledger itself is thread-local.

## Verification

See [Development status](development-status.md) for verification scope. A fit whose shape is not verified within `tau` emits a `RuntimeWarning` by default; check `fit_diagnostics` before quantitative use.
